Why won't the elbow match?

The recovered cost reproduces the human's tool path and press force — but the redundant elbow sits about six degrees off. Is that the cost failing, or something else entirely? A short case study in telling those two apart.

A redundant arm can reach the same tool pose many ways. Our recovered cost lands the deployed scrape on the demonstration's rail and force almost exactly — yet the elbow deploys near 56° while the human holds it near 50°. The tempting conclusion is that the cost simply doesn't explain the posture. Before accepting that, it's worth asking a sharper question: can the controller even express the demo's posture cheaply?

A reusable test: interrogate the rollout cloud

Rather than argue from the single deployed trajectory, we dump the controller's full sample cloud — every one of ~1000 rollouts — and ask three questions of the demo posture:

  1. Coverage — does the cloud ever reach the demo elbow?
  2. Selection — if it's reachable, does the cost pick it?
  3. Cause — if not, which feature is voting it down?

What it isn't

Three plausible culprits fall away under the cloud:

Units. The IRL optimised σ-normalised features while the sampler selected on raw ones — a real confound that muted small features. Fixed; the elbow moved, but not far.
Coverage. The cloud spans 48–60°; the demo at 50° is well inside it.
Jerk. Low-elbow samples are, if anything, smoother — and smoothness doesn't predict cost.

What it is: the tool leaves the rail

Ranking every feature by its correlation with the elbow and with cost points at one cluster — and it's not effort or smoothness, it's contact geometry:

Flexing the elbow drives the tool off the rail. corr(elbow, rail deviation) = −0.66, and press-force and approach-gap light up in lockstep — because all three are the same fault: the tool no longer sitting on the stick. The cost is right to penalise those samples. The controller just can't flex the elbow without leaving the rail.

The fix, validated in miniature

The leak has a clean cause: the controller resolves redundancy with a null-space basis frozen at one posture, so a wide posture offset is only perpendicular-to-task to first order — a finite offset bends the tool off the rail. Re-projecting the tool back onto the rail at runtime (a Gauss–Newton "reanchor") removes the leak. A pure-numpy toy — a 3-link arm on a rail — reproduces the whole mechanism in fifteen seconds:

Why it matters

The posture gap is a geometric artifact of redundancy resolution, not a limit of the recovered cost. That distinction is the whole point: "the cost doesn't explain the posture" and "the controller can't express the posture on-rail" look identical from the deployed trajectory, and opposite from the cloud. Reading the cloud — coverage, selection, cause — is how you tell which one you're holding.

Status — the reanchor is confirmed on the toy and its mechanism is measured on the full arm's sample cloud; the on-arm confirmation run (does the whole contact cluster decouple, and how much residual effort-preference remains) is in progress.
